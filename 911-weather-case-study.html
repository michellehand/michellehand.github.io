Public systems case study

911 calls in a changing weather context.

A civic data project using Seattle 911 dispatch records, historical weather observations, and time-series features to predict daily traffic-related emergency call volume.

The goal is not to claim that weather causes emergencies. The goal is to test whether environmental and temporal context can improve operational visibility for public safety planning.

Research question

Does weather help explain emergency demand?

This project narrows the question to traffic-related 911 calls in Seattle. Traffic demand is shaped by time, enforcement patterns, commute behavior, events, seasonality, and weather. The analysis tests whether weather features add value after accounting for calendar and recent-volume patterns.

Primary question

Can temperature, precipitation, and seasonal weather flags improve prediction of daily traffic-related 911 call volume?

Operational use case

Could weather-aware forecasting support staffing, preparedness, or public safety resource planning?

Analytical caution

Weather is treated as context, not a single-cause explanation. The strongest signal came from recent call history, not weather alone.

Data design

From dispatch logs to model-ready features.

The workflow starts with 2024–2026 Seattle 911 dispatch records, filters to traffic-related events, aggregates them into daily call volume, then joins daily weather observations and engineered time-series features.

Method

A four-notebook analytical workflow.

The case study is organized as a reproducible pipeline: data exploration, weather merge, feature engineering, and modeling.

Explore Seattle 911 calls

Loaded 1,340,940 records, standardized timestamps, reviewed event groups, and identified traffic as a high-volume call category.

Aggregate traffic demand

Created a daily traffic-call table and inspected spikes, seasonality, weekday patterns, and anomalous June 2025 traffic surges.

Join weather context

Merged daily traffic counts to Seattle weather observations and created precipitation, temperature, rain, hot-day, and freezing-day indicators.

Compare model families

Tested baseline linear regression, weather-enhanced linear regression, random forest with weather, and random forest with weather plus lag features.

Findings

Weather mattered, but recent demand mattered more.

The strongest model was not purely weather-driven. Weather and calendar features helped, but the largest improvement came from adding lagged call volume and a 7-day rolling average.

Daily demand is noisy but patterned

Seattle traffic-related calls varied substantially day to day, with visible seasonal movement and short-term spikes.

Limitations

What this analysis cannot claim.

  • Weather may correlate with activity patterns rather than directly causing incidents.
  • Daily aggregation can hide hour-level spikes.
  • Call volume reflects reporting and dispatch patterns, not every incident that occurred.
  • The June 2025 spike appeared tied to moving-violation activity rather than weather alone.

Next steps

Move from model prototype to operational dashboard.

Next iterations could add hourly weather, holiday/event flags, spatial features, call subcategories, and a lightweight dashboard that shows expected traffic-call demand by day.
